Part One

Safety evidence decays under change.

Modern devices are software-defined systems. Their safety case lives across many tools — and quietly drifts out of alignment every time the design moves.

01
01 The shape of the problem

Software is becoming the medical device.

Devices are now connected, configurable, workflow-driven cyber-physical systems. Safety is an emergent property of behavior and architecture — not of any single requirement.

Connected

Cloud services, OTA updates, telemetry, fleet data — new failure and exposure paths.

Software behavior

Modes, alarms, configuration, UI workflows — where most hazards actually live.

AI / automation

Inference, assistance, model drift — added review and assurance burden.

Device platform

Sensors, actuators, real-time embedded control, hardware safety.

Opening question: as software complexity grows, how do we keep safety continuously assured under change?
02 A familiar story

It is 11 p.m. before an audit. An engineer changes one line in REQ-145. The next morning, no one in the room can answer the question that follows:

“What evidence is still valid?”

A small change · a clean diff · a safety case that quietly stopped meaning what it said
03 Why it matters

Drift is expensive — and quiet.

Unverified change is the silent driver of recalls, rework, and audit findings. The cost is not the broken link — it is the doubt every broken link injects into the case.

~21%
FDA recalls

Software & traceability are flagged in roughly one-fifth of Class II/III recalls.

~40%
Rework cost

Late requirement and risk rework absorbs 30–45% of typical V&V budgets.

Days
Audit time

Reconstructing a single broken trace before audit takes days, not minutes.

↻
Lost confidence

Each silent break weakens the safety case the next reviewer reads.

The underlying problem: loss of evidence integrity under change — claims, architecture, behavior, controls, and verification falling out of sync as the product evolves.
04 Where it breaks

Change is where the problem becomes operational.

After a code or config change, teams must decide what to re-verify. They over-verify (cost) or under-verify (missed safety impact) — because the same triggers recur across the V-Model.

Allocation

Paper controls

Risk controls exist on paper but aren't anchored to a component, interface, or behavior step — hard to verify “where implemented”.

Granularity

Hand-wavy trace

Hazards are high-level while design and tests are detailed; trace becomes ambiguous and easy to misinterpret.

Emergence

Coupling & behavior

Timing, modes, alarms, and faults interact to create hazards invisible from isolated requirements.

Fragmentation

Tool silos

Different tools own risk, requirements, design, and tests; semantics aren't shared, so inconsistencies persist.

Coverage

Scenarios not first-class

Edge cases and degraded modes live in prose; negative-scenario coverage can't be defended.

Freshness

Stale safety case

Risk, intent, and verification diverge over time; change impact is assessed manually and late.

Ten recurring triggers, one root cause: meaning between artifacts is implicit, so it cannot be checked when the design moves.
05 The semantic gap

Traceability exists — but not enough meaning.

A matrix can say two items are connected. It rarely says why, how, where implemented, or whether the evidence is now stale.

Trace row

Requirement → Test

The relationship exists, but the safety meaning lives outside the model.

Missing: why this test proves this safety claim.

Allocation

Control → Architecture

The control may not be allocated to a behavior, component, interface, or boundary.

Missing: where implemented and who owns it.

Freshness

Evidence → Baseline

Verification goes stale when assumptions, flows, or interfaces change.

Missing: deterministic re-review scope.

The gap is semantic, not graphical. The goal is typed, checkable safety relationships — not more arrows.
06 The reviewer's question

Every review reduces to one question.

“Is the device still safe after all the changes?” To answer it, reviewers ask for one complete safety thread, end-to-end.

For each important hazard, show…
  • The risk control that reduces the risk.
  • Exactly where it is implemented — which component or interface is responsible.
  • The verification evidence that proves it works, with acceptance criteria.
  • The behavior it acts on — the flow, mode, alarm path, or edge-case scenario.
And when something changes…
  • What changed, and what part of the system and safety case it impacts.
  • Why the proposed re-verification scope is sufficient — not excessive, not risky.
  • That the evidence corresponds to the exact released configuration.
  • The approvals tied to that specific baseline.
The safety thread is the unit of assurance. If we can make that object typed and computable, the rest follows.
Part Two

Make the safety argument source code.

Architecture is the natural backbone for the safety story. Treat it as code, and review questions become executable checks.

02
07 The backbone

Architecture can connect the safety story.

Architecture is the natural integration point between clinical intent, risk controls, requirements, behavior, interfaces, verification, and released evidence.

Intent

Use + assumptions

What the device must do, for whom, in which setting.

Risk

Hazards + controls

What must be prevented, and how it is mitigated.

Architecture

The living backbone

Responsibilities, interfaces, behavior, and change-impact paths.

Evidence

Verification + DHF

What proves the claim for this released baseline.

Architecture is not just a picture. It can become the computable backbone of safety assurance.
08 Borrowed discipline

Medical can learn from architecture-centric domains.

Aerospace and automotive have stronger traditions of views, allocation, interface contracts, and model-based reasoning. MEMO adapts them to medical realities — without the heavyweight cost.

Aerospace

Allocation chains

Architecture and allocation connect mission, design, safety analysis (ARP4761), and verification evidence.

Automotive

Platforms + contracts

ISO 26262-style mechanisms standardize safety patterns, interfaces, and reuse.

Medical devices

Process-rich, architecture-light

Strong QMS and traceability, but variable architecture maturity and high MBSE setup cost.

The goal is not heavyweight MBSE. It is architecture-centered discipline with code-first adoption friction.
09 The missing layer

What is needed is a shared, code-first model.

A low-cost, versionable, architecture-backed model that carries engineering meaning across teams — and is checkable by rules.

Input

Text-first source

  • Diffable, reviewable, versioned in git.
  • An architecture backbone for risk, requirements, behavior, evidence.
  • A prefab methodology — not a blank canvas.
Core

Meaning + structure + rules

  • Typed entities and relationships.
  • Responsibilities, interfaces, scenarios.
  • Explicit change-impact paths.
Output

Checked, generated views

  • Compiler-style warnings, errors, closure.
  • Viewpoints: risk, architecture, DHF, cyber.
  • Generated review and audit artifacts.
The goal: MBSE benefits with code-first adoption and far lower setup friction.
10 Architecture as code

The model is source code for the safety argument.

A safety thread, expressed as SysML v2 text in the memo:: namespace — diffable, identifiable, and machine-readable.

examples/gpca-pump/model/gpca_risk.sysml

  attribute id = "HZ-001";
  attribute hazardType = HazardTypeKind::drugDeliveryError;
  attribute severity   = CriticalityKind::catastrophic;
}

part lockoutControl : RiskControl {
  attribute id = "RC-001";
  attribute controlKind = RiskControlKind::inherentSafeDesign;
}
examples/gpca-pump/model/gpca_requirements.sysml
requirement reqLockout : SoftwareRequirement {
  attribute id = "REQ-025";
  attribute statement =
    "Ignore patient bolus requests that
     violate lockout or max bolus.";
  attribute sourceKind =
    RequirementSourceKind::risk;
  attribute safetyClass = SafetyClassKind::C;
}
Stable identifiers · typed relationships · diffable evidence. The safety case now lives in normal engineering workflows.
11 Compile the argument

We compile code. We should compile the safety argument.

Architecture review questions become executable checks. MEMO ships native KerML closure constraints traced to ISO 14971, IEC 62304, and ISO 13485.

terminal — closure & consistency
$ memo validate

CR-MED-001  Hazard must have ≥1 risk control   (ISO 14971)
            Missing mitigation: hazAirInLine

CR-MED-003  Risk control must be verified      (ISO 14971 §7.4)
            Missing verification: rcDoorOpenAlarm

CR-MED-011  SW requirement must derive from a
            system requirement                (IEC 62304)

Result: 2 errors · 1 warning · safety thread HZ-001 closed
Why it matters
  • Find gaps before design review or audit.
  • Make closure visible to software, systems, risk, and V&V at once.
  • Run the rules in CI before merge or release.
  • Turn each change into a deterministic re-review scope.
The memorable hook: safety evidence becomes something engineers can validate, like a build.

17 memo::core

A small core: bases, enums, links.

The whole ontology specializes a handful of abstract bases. Enumerations encode categorical attributes; links carry typed semantics.

core/memo_common.sysml
part def IdentifiedElement {
  attribute id : String;
  attribute name : String;
  attribute description : String;
}
part def TraceableElement :> IdentifiedElement {
  attribute rationale : String;
  attribute sourceReference : String;
}
part def ArchitectureElement :> TraceableElement;
part def VerifiableElement   :> TraceableElement;
part def EvidenceElement     :> TraceableElement;
57 enumerations

ConcernKind · SafetyClassKind · HazardTypeKind · RiskControlKind · ThreatCategoryKind · AudienceKind · WorkflowStageKind …

Semantic links

SemanticLink → RequirementSatisfactionLink, VerificationLink, FunctionAllocationLink, and more.

Specialize, don't edit

Every domain element :> specializes a base. Add kinds; never fork the core.

Categorical meaning is enumerated, structural meaning is typed. That is what makes the model checkable.

19 Risk & security

Safety and security as peer layers.

A hazard chain on one side, a threat chain on the other — both feed the same RequirementDriver / VerifiableElement contract.

architecture/memo_risk.sysml
part def Hazard :> TraceableElement {
  attribute hazardType : HazardTypeKind;
  attribute foreseeable : Boolean;
}
part def RiskControl :> VerifiableElement {
  attribute controlKind : RiskControlKind;
}
part def RiskAfterMitigation :> Risk {
  attribute residualAcceptability : String;
}
architecture/memo_cybersecurity.sysml
part def CybersecurityAsset :> ArchitectureElement {
  attribute assetKind : AssetKind;
  attribute safetyRelevant : Boolean;
}
part def Threat :> RequirementDriver {
  attribute threatCategory : ThreatCategoryKind;
  attribute attackVector : String;
}
// Threat :> RequirementDriver — exactly like Risk.
Why peers: because Threat drives requirements just like Risk does, the same downstream machinery generates security requirements and verification.
20 Semantic links

Links carry roles — not just direction.

Every link is a SemanticLink subtype with named part roles and a link status. The linker checks the element kind on each end.

21 Method & views

Methodology shapes how the ontology is applied.

Definitions select rigor, viewpoints, rules, patterns, and gates. Viewpoints declare intent (ISO 42010); views bind that intent to selected content.

memo::methodology
  • core — MethodologyDefinition · ResolvedMethodology · ProjectMethodBinding
  • profiles — light · balanced · formal rigor instances
  • rules — ViewRule (required · recommended · forbidden)
  • patterns · workflow · gates — recurring shapes, stage sequencing, ReviewGate exit criteria
memo::viewpoints + memo::views
  • Viewpoint = intent: audience, stage, allowed element & relationship kinds.
  • View = bound content: a ViewSelectionQuery over layers and concerns.
  • First-class libraries — not buried under methodology or compliance.
  • Default viewpoints per audience and workflow stage.
Separation of concerns: intent and content are distinct, so the same model serves the safety, architecture, V&V, and regulatory reviewers.
22 Extension

Extend through methodology profiles.

Pick rigor, pick viewpoints, author rules, bind to a project, add your own domain kinds. The core ontology stays unchanged.

1
Pick rigor · MethodRigorKind = lightweight | balanced | formal
2
Pick viewpoints · audience = safetyEngineer · stage = risk
3
Author rules · ViewRule · elementTypeName = "Hazard" · strength = required
4
Bind to project · ProjectMethodBinding · projectName = "GPCA Pump"
5
Add domain kinds · part def InfusionMode :> ModeState { … } in your own package
The rule: add packages and methodology profiles — don't edit core. Methodology guides without owning viewpoints.

23 Worked example

Meet GPCA — memo::examples::gpca

A Generic Patient-Controlled Analgesia infusion pump — the public-domain U. Minnesota CriSys benchmark, IEC 62304 Class C.

Why GPCA
  • University of Minnesota CriSys benchmark, public REQ catalogue (REQ 1–86).
  • IEC 62304 Class C software with a full ISO 14971 hazard chain.
  • Cited across FDA, NIH, and INCOSE literature.
  • Real instances in the repo — not pseudo-code.
What is modeled
  • Stakeholders, intended use, needs & software requirements.
  • Hierarchical mode machine — OFF · IDLE · PAUSED · BASAL · SQUARE · PBOLUS; per-subsystem function decomposition.
  • SW components (Top_Level_Mode, Alarm, Infusion_Manager …) with period / deadline / WCET.
  • Hazard chain + lockout control + benefit-risk eval; STRIDE threat model; AGREE-like & LTL properties.
One model, every layer: requirements, architecture, interfaces, behavior, risk, cyber, verification, trace, and document views.
